4.2 Randomisation and Replication

Randomisation is the process of randomly assigning treatments to experimental units. It eliminates systematic bias and ensures results are representative of the population.

Replication is the use of multiple experimental units per treatment. It increases the reliability and precision of estimates, and allows you to separate true treatment effects from random variation.

4.3 Understanding Experimental Design; the case study of vegatation.

Today we will test our understanding of expeirmental design by looking at a case study of the effect of vegetation management treatment on plant species richness in a lowland grassland.

Researchers have 20 grassland plots and want to test the effect of four different treatment on plant species richness. The treatments are:

The four treatments are:

  • T1: Unmanaged control
  • T2: Autumn cutting
  • T3: Spring burning
  • T4: Sheep grazing

They can only test one treatment per plot, and they want to have 5 replicates of each treatment, so they need to know how they can decide which treatment goes in which plot. This will need to be done randomly.

4.3.1 Completely Randomised Design (CRD)

In a completely randomised design (CRD), treatments are assigned to experimental units purely at random, with no restrictions or grouping. It is the simplest valid experimental design.

The treatment list is completely shuffled at random and assigned to plots. I will show you how this can be done in R code.

In R, the sample() function randomly shuffles the treatment list, and set.seed() ensures the result is reproducible.

4.3.2 Blocked Design

In a blocked design, the experimental area is divided into blocks that are as homogeneous as possible. Within each block, treatments are randomly assigned to the plots. This approach helps to control for variability due to environmental gradients or other factors that might affect the response variable.

In plain terms: think of the field as being divided into 5 strips (the repeats), each containing 4 plots (the treatments). Within each strip, every treatment appears exactly once — but the order is randomised. So instead of scattering all 20 plots randomly across the whole field, we say:

“In strip 1, one plot gets Control, one gets Autumn Cut, one gets Spring Burn, and one gets Sheep Grazing — but we randomly decide which plot gets which.”

Then we do the same for strips 2, 3, 4 and 5.

This guarantees that every treatment appears in every part of the field. If there is a moisture gradient running across the field, each treatment will experience a range of conditions — rather than one treatment accidentally getting all the wet plots.

The key advantage of a blocked design is that any differences between blocks — such as a moisture gradient running across the field — are accounted for in the analysis rather than absorbed into the error. This makes it much easier to detect true treatment effects. However, the blocked design cannot deal with trends across the experimental area. 4.3.3 The Latin Square Design (LSD)

The Latin Square Design (LSD) is a more complex design that can account for trends in two directions. Imagine that as well as a moisture gradient running left to right across the field, there is also a temperature gradient running top to bottom. A blocked design can control for one of these — but not both at the same time.

The Latin Square Design solves this by controlling for variation in two directions simultaneously — across rows and across columns. Every treatment appears exactly once in each row and exactly once in each column

Note

The trade-off: moving to a Latin Square means reducing the number of plots from 20 to 16 in our example. You also need the number of treatments to equal the number of rows and columns, which limits flexibility.

lsd_plan <- data.frame(
  col       = rep(1:4, times = 4),
  row       = rep(4:1, each = 4),
  treatment = c("Control",      "Autumn Cut",   "Spring Burn",  "Sheep Grazing",
                "Autumn Cut",   "Control",      "Sheep Grazing","Spring Burn",
                "Spring Burn",  "Sheep Grazing","Control",      "Autumn Cut",
                "Sheep Grazing","Spring Burn",  "Autumn Cut",   "Control")
)

4.3.4 Split Plot Design

Sometimes practical constraints make full randomisation impossible. For example, imagine that our plots are 3m × 3m, but the equipment needed to apply a treatment covers a minimum area of 12m × 3m. We cannot apply a different treatment to each small plot individually — the equipment is simply too large.

Instead, we apply one factor across the larger 12m × 3m area (the whole plot), and then subdivide it into four smaller 3m × 3m plots (the split plots) within which a second factor is randomised.

In plain terms:

One factor is applied to large areas because it has to be — the other is randomised within those large areas at a finer scale.

split_plan <- data.frame(
  col        = rep(1:4, times = 4),
  row        = rep(4:1, each = 4),
  whole_plot = rep(c("A", "B"), each = 8),
  sub_plot   = c(sample(1:4), sample(1:4),
                 sample(1:4), sample(1:4))
)

4.3.5 Repeated Measures Design

A repeated measures design is used when measurements are taken from the same experimental unit more than once — for example, measuring the growth of an animal at several points throughout its life.

In plain terms:

Rather than measuring different animals at each time point, you follow the same animals over time.

This matters because observations taken from the same individual are not independent of each other — an animal that is large at week 1 is likely to still be large at week 4. The analysis must account for both the treatment effect and the sequence of measurements over time.

Note

Why does this matter? Ignoring the repeated nature of the data and treating each time point as independent would inflate your sample size artificially and lead to unreliable results.

Why must a repeated measures design be analysed differently from a standard design?

4.4 Analysis of Variance (ANOVA)

4.4.1 What is ANOVA?

So far we have used t-tests to compare the means of two groups. But what if we want to compare more than two groups at once? In our grassland experiment, we have four treatments — Control, Autumn Cut, Spring Burn, and Sheep Grazing. When we have more than two groups to compare, we use ANOVA (Analysis of Variance).

ANOVA asks one overarching question:

“Is there enough evidence to suggest that at least one treatment produces a different mean response from the others?”

4.4.2 The Null Hypothesis

Like all statistical tests, ANOVA starts with a null hypothesis — a statement that there is no effect or difference. In the context of ANOVA, the null hypothesis states that:

All treatment means are equal.

If the probability of this being true is less than 1 in 20 (p < 0.05), we reject the null hypothesis and conclude that at least one treatment mean is different from the others.

4.4.3 How Does ANOVA Work?

ANOVA works by comparing two types of variation in the data:

1. Within-group variance — the background noise

Imagine you have 5 Control plots. You would expect them all to have a similar number of plant species, because they all received the same treatment. But in reality they will not be identical — one plot might have 8 species, another 10, another 7. This is the natural variation that exists even though the treatment was the same.

Within-group variance measures how different plots are from each other within the same treatment group — the natural background noise that exists regardless of treatment.

To calculate this, we look at how far each plot’s value is from its group mean, square those differences (so that negative and positive differences do not cancel each other out), and add them all up. This is called the within-group sum of squares.

In plain terms:

For each plot, ask: “how far is this plot from the average for its treatment group?” Square that distance, then add all those squared distances together.

A large within-group sum of squares means plots within the same treatment group are very different from each other — a lot of background noise. A small within-group sum of squares means plots within the same treatment group are very similar to each other — very little background noise.

2. Between-group variance — the treatment signal

Now imagine comparing the average species richness across the four treatment groups. If the treatments have no effect, these averages should all be similar. If treatments do have an effect, the averages will differ more than you would expect from background noise alone.

Between-group variance measures how different the treatment group means are from each other — the signal we are hoping to detect.

In plain terms: > If Sheep Grazing plots have on average 12 species and Control plots have on average 8 species, that difference contributes to the between-group variance.

4.4.4 The F-ratio

ANOVA combines these two estimates into a single value called the F-ratio:

F = \frac{\text{Between-group variance}}{\text{Within-group variance}}

Think of it as a signal-to-noise ratio. Here is what the F-ratio tells us:

  • If the between-group variance and within-group variance are similar in size, it will be difficult to detect significant differences between treatments — the treatment signal is being drowned out by background noise. In this case, F will be close to 1.
  • If the within-group variance is small relative to the between-group variance, the treatment signal stands out clearly above the noise, and significant differences are more likely to be detected. In this case, F will be much greater than 1.

In plain terms:

The larger the F-ratio, the more convincing the evidence that at least one treatment is producing a genuinely different result.

For a given value of F, and for a given number of treatments and replicates, we can calculate the probability that the null hypothesis is true. As before, our threshold is 1 in 20 — if p < 0.05, we reject the null hypothesis and conclude that at least one treatment mean is significantly different from the others.

Note

Important: a significant ANOVA result tells you that at least one treatment mean is different — but it does not tell you which treatment differs from which. For that, you need a post-hoc test, which we will cover shortly.

4.4.5 Running a One-Way ANOVA in R

A one-way ANOVA tests the effect of one factor (in our case, treatment) on a response variable (species richness). In R, we use the aov() function:

# Simulate some species richness data for illustration
set.seed(42)

species_data <- data.frame(
  treatment = rep(c("Control", "Autumn Cut", "Spring Burn", "Sheep Grazing"), 
                  each = 5),
  richness  = c(rnorm(5, mean = 8,  sd = 2),
                rnorm(5, mean = 12, sd = 2),
                rnorm(5, mean = 10, sd = 2),
                rnorm(5, mean = 14, sd = 2))
)

# Run the ANOVA
anova_result <- aov(richness ~ treatment, data = species_data)
summary(anova_result)
            Df Sum Sq Mean Sq F value Pr(>F)  
treatment    3  59.76  19.919    2.91 0.0666 .
Residuals   16 109.51   6.845                 
---
Signif. codes:  0 '***' 0.001 '**' 0.01 '*' 0.05 '.' 0.1 ' ' 1

The summary() output shows:

  • Df — degrees of freedom
  • Sum Sq — the sum of squares (a measure of total variation)
  • Mean Sq — the mean square (sum of squares divided by degrees of freedom)
  • F value — the F-ratio
  • Pr(>F) — the p-value

If the p-value is less than 0.05, at least one treatment mean is significantly different from the others.

Tip

Reminder: ANOVA is a parametric test, so the same assumptions apply as for the t-test — the data within each group should be approximately normally distributed, and the variances should be approximately equal across groups. Check these with the Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene’s test before running ANOVA.

### The Latin Square Design (LSD)
The Latin Square Design (LSD) is a more complex design that can account for trends in two directions. Imagine that as well as a moisture gradient running left to right across the field, there is also a temperature gradient running top to bottom. A blocked design can control for one of these — but not both at the same time.

The **Latin Square Design** solves this by controlling for variation in **two directions simultaneously** — across rows *and* across columns. Every treatment appears exactly once in each row and exactly once in each column

::: {.callout-note}
**The trade-off:** moving to a Latin Square means reducing the number of plots from 20 to 16 in our example. You also need the number of treatments to equal the number 
of rows and columns, which limits flexibility.
:::

### Split Plot Design

Sometimes practical constraints make full randomisation impossible. For example, imagine that our plots are **3m × 3m**, but the equipment needed to apply a treatment covers a minimum area of **12m × 3m**. We cannot apply a different treatment to each small plot individually — the equipment is simply too large.

Instead, we apply one factor across the larger **12m × 3m** area (the **whole plot**), and then subdivide it into four smaller **3m × 3m** plots (the **split plots**) within which a second factor is randomised.

In plain terms:

> *One factor is applied to large areas because it has to be — the other is randomised within those large areas at a finer scale.*

### Repeated Measures Design

A **repeated measures design** is used when measurements are taken from the **same experimental unit more than once** — for example, measuring the growth of an 
animal at several points throughout its life.

In plain terms:

> *Rather than measuring different animals at each time point, you follow the same animals over time.*

This matters because observations taken from the same individual are not independent of each other — an animal that is large at week 1 is likely to still be large at 
week 4. The analysis must account for both the **treatment effect** and the **sequence of measurements over time**.

::: {.callout-note}
**Why does this matter?** Ignoring the repeated nature of the data and treating each time point as independent would inflate your sample size artificially and lead to 
unreliable results.
:::

**Why must a repeated measures design be analysed differently from a standard design?**

`r longmcq(c("Because there are too many data points", answer = "Because observations from the same individual are not independent — the analysis must account for both the treatment effect and the temporal sequence of measurements", "Because the treatments change over time", "Because replication is not possible in repeated measures"))`

| Design | Controls for | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| CRD | Nothing | Plots are uniform |
| RCBD | Variation in one direction | One known gradient |
| LSD | Variation in two directions | Two known gradients |
| Split plot | Practical constraints on randomisation | Equipment or logistics limit full randomisation |
| Repeated measures | Non-independence of repeated observations | Same unit measured multiple times |

## Analysis of Variance (ANOVA)

### What is ANOVA?

So far we have used t-tests to compare the means of **two groups**. But what if we want to compare **more than two groups** at once? In our grassland experiment, we have four treatments — Control, Autumn Cut, Spring Burn, and Sheep Grazing. When we have more than two groups to compare, we use **ANOVA** (Analysis of Variance).

ANOVA asks one overarching question:

> *"Is there enough evidence to suggest that at least one treatment produces a different mean response from the others?"*

### The Null Hypothesis

Like all statistical tests, ANOVA starts with a **null hypothesis** — a statement that there is no effect or difference. In the context of ANOVA, the null hypothesis states that:

> *All treatment means are equal.*

If the probability of this being true is less than 1 in 20 (p < 0.05), we reject the null hypothesis and conclude that at least one treatment mean is different from the others.

### How Does ANOVA Work?

ANOVA works by comparing two types of variation in the data:

---

**1. Within-group variance — the background noise**

Imagine you have 5 Control plots. You would expect them all to have a similar number of plant species, because they all received the same treatment. But in reality they will not be identical — one plot might have 8 species, another 10, another 7. This is the natural variation that exists even though the treatment was the same.

> *Within-group variance measures how different plots are from each other within the same treatment group — the natural background noise that exists regardless of treatment.*

To calculate this, we look at how far each plot's value is from its **group mean**, square those differences (so that negative and positive differences do not cancel each other out), and add them all up. This is called the **within-group sum of squares**.

In plain terms:

> *For each plot, ask: "how far is this plot from the average for its treatment group?" Square that distance, then add all those squared distances together.*

A large within-group sum of squares means plots within the same treatment group are very different from each other — a lot of background noise. A small within-group sum of squares means plots within the same treatment group are very similar to each other — very little background noise.


**2. Between-group variance — the treatment signal**

Now imagine comparing the **average** species richness across the four treatment groups. If the treatments have no effect, these averages should all be similar. If treatments do have an effect, the averages will differ more than you would expect from background noise alone.

> *Between-group variance measures how different the treatment group means are from each other — the signal we are hoping to detect.*

In plain terms:
> *If Sheep Grazing plots have on average 12 species and Control plots have on average 8 species, that difference contributes to the between-group variance.*

### The F-ratio

ANOVA combines these two estimates into a single value called the **F-ratio**:

$$F = \frac{\text{Between-group variance}}{\text{Within-group variance}}$$

Think of it as a **signal-to-noise ratio**. Here is what the F-ratio tells us:

- If the between-group variance and within-group variance are **similar in size**, it will be difficult to detect significant differences between treatments — the treatment signal is being drowned out by background noise. In this case, F will be close to **1**.
- If the within-group variance is **small relative to** the between-group variance, the treatment signal stands out clearly above the noise, and significant differences are more likely to be detected. In this case, F will be **much greater than 1**.

In plain terms:

> *The larger the F-ratio, the more convincing the evidence that at least one treatment is producing a genuinely different result.*

For a given value of F, and for a given number of treatments and replicates, we can calculate the probability that the null hypothesis is true. As before, our threshold is 1 in 20 — if p < 0.05, we reject the null hypothesis and conclude that at least one treatment mean is significantly different from the others.

::: {.callout-note}
**Important:** a significant ANOVA result tells you that *at least one* treatment mean is different — but it does not tell you *which* treatment differs from which. For that, you need a **post-hoc test**, which we will cover shortly.
:::

### Running a One-Way ANOVA in R

A one-way ANOVA tests the effect of **one factor** (in our case, treatment) on a response variable (species richness). In R, we use the `aov()` function:

`r hide("Click to show: R code for one-way ANOVA")`

```{r anova-example}
# Simulate some species richness data for illustration
set.seed(42)

species_data <- data.frame(
  treatment = rep(c("Control", "Autumn Cut", "Spring Burn", "Sheep Grazing"), 
                  each = 5),
  richness  = c(rnorm(5, mean = 8,  sd = 2),
                rnorm(5, mean = 12, sd = 2),
                rnorm(5, mean = 10, sd = 2),
                rnorm(5, mean = 14, sd = 2))
)

# Run the ANOVA
anova_result <- aov(richness ~ treatment, data = species_data)
summary(anova_result)
```

`r unhide()`

The `summary()` output shows:

- **Df** — degrees of freedom
- **Sum Sq** — the sum of squares (a measure of total variation)
- **Mean Sq** — the mean square (sum of squares divided by degrees of freedom)
- **F value** — the F-ratio
- **Pr(>F)** — the p-value

If the p-value is less than 0.05, at least one treatment mean is significantly 
different from the others.

::: {.callout-tip}
**Reminder:** ANOVA is a parametric test, so the same assumptions apply as for the 
t-test — the data within each group should be approximately normally distributed, and 
the variances should be approximately equal across groups. Check these with the 
Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene's test before running ANOVA.
:::
